## Name the symptom without diagnosing the part

Start with what you can see, hear or observe: weak return flow, bubbles at a return, water level dropping, pump cycling, grinding or humming, heater display blank, automation offline, cloudy water or debris returning after cleaning. Add whether the symptom is constant, only appears during a schedule, follows rain, begins after startup or changes when another feature runs.

Avoid converting that observation into “bad motor,” “air leak” or “failed board” unless a qualified person already documented it. One symptom can have several causes, and the same piece of equipment may serve different pool functions. ENERGY STAR notes that pump speeds vary by operation; filtration and cleaner operation can require different flow. That is one reason the current mode and schedule belong in the brief.

- What you see or hear
- When it happens
- Which mode is running
- Whether the pattern is constant or intermittent

## Capture three safe views of the system

Take one wide photo of the pool that shows water level and general clarity, one wide photo of the complete equipment pad, and one closer photo of the relevant display, leak area or label. Stand on a dry, stable surface. Keep hands away from wet electrical equipment, moving parts and open panels. Do not remove a pump lid or equipment cover solely to improve the picture.

CPSC identifies pumps, filters, vacuums, lights, outlets and switches as electrical sources around pools and explains that wet surfaces increase shock risk. If there is tingling in the water, flickering underwater lighting or another possible electrical event, keep swimmers out and follow emergency safety guidance rather than continuing a content-driven troubleshooting session.

- Pool overview
- Equipment-pad overview
- Display, label or visible leak detail
- Dry position and closed covers

## Create a 60-second equipment inventory

Photograph the manufacturer and model labels for the pump, filter, heater or heat pump, chlorination equipment and automation controller involved. Record any error code exactly as displayed, including punctuation and whether it clears after a normal scheduled cycle. If there are multiple pumps, label the photo by function only if you know it; otherwise use “left,” “center” and “right.”

Add the current schedule visible in the app or controller, the approximate equipment age if documented and the date of the last professional service. Never include passwords, Wi-Fi credentials, gate codes or account tokens in public photos. Those details belong in a verified private handoff after the provider confirms the channel.

- Manufacturer and model
- Exact displayed code
- Current schedule or mode
- Last documented service date
- No credentials in screenshots

## Add the water-and-weather timeline

Write the date the symptom began, recent heavy rain or storm activity, a notable water-level change, recent chemical or cleaning service, a power outage, a schedule change and any equipment work. If you have owner-operated test results, record the date, time and method without presenting them as a professional water analysis.

The timeline helps separate a single event from a repeating pattern. It also keeps the request honest: “water became cloudy the morning after the storm and the pump now shuts off after ten minutes” is more useful than “the filter is broken.” Do not add chemicals or change several settings merely to create more data before the visit.

- First observed date
- Rain, power or water-level change
- Recent service or chemical addition
- Settings changed
- Pattern after each cycle

## Frequently asked questions

### What photos help with a pool repair request?

Take a pool overview, a complete equipment-pad overview and a detail of the display, model label or visible leak from a dry, stable position. Keep covers closed and do not touch wet electrical equipment or moving parts to improve the photo.

### Should I diagnose the pump before calling?

No. Describe the sound, flow, display, timing and operating mode. Different pool functions can use different pump speeds, and one symptom may have several causes. Let the qualified service professional evaluate the system and confirm the repair scope.

### How do I verify a Florida pool contractor license?

Use the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation license search. Search the license number and confirm the licensee and business entity relationship. The correct category depends on the work, so ask the provider which scope applies.

### What if I suspect an electrical problem around the pool?

Keep people out of the water and away from wet electrical equipment. CPSC advises turning off power in a shock emergency only when it can be done safely and calling 911. Use qualified electrical or pool professionals for inspection; do not open energized panels for a content checklist.
