# New Pet Patient Visit in Suntree: What to Prepare Before You Request

A continuity-of-care packet for records, medication labels, observation timelines and appointment confirmation.

> Unofficial editorial concept prepared by EB28. Nothing has been approved, scheduled or published for Rubio Pet Hospital.

Changing veterinary practices or establishing care for a new pet creates a continuity problem: the owner knows the story, the previous clinic holds part of the record and the new team needs enough accurate context to decide what to ask next. A useful web page can reduce that friction before the appointment request begins.

## Quick answer: how to prepare for new pet patient visit in Suntree

Before requesting a new-patient veterinary visit, ask the prior clinic to send the complete relevant record, then make a one-page handoff with the pet’s identifiers, current medications, known reactions, the reason for the visit, a dated observation timeline and your three priorities. Confirm that the new hospital received the records and request, then wait for accepted appointment details and clinic-specific preparation instructions. Do not use the request as an emergency channel.

- Keep the original record and your one-page summary separate.
- Photograph medication and supplement labels.
- List observations by date, frequency and context.
- Confirm record receipt, appointment acceptance and arrival instructions.

## Create a continuity packet, not a rewritten medical record

Request the relevant medical and surgical history, vaccination history, laboratory results, imaging reports, discharge instructions and prior-clinic contact information. Keep those original records intact. Your one-page handoff should point to them, not edit them into a homemade clinical summary that loses author, date or context.

AAHA referral guidance calls for records, diagnostic results and imaging to be delivered through an agreed method. It also notes that the receiving team reviews records to reduce unnecessary duplication. Even when the visit is not a specialty referral, the same continuity principle applies: the new hospital should receive source records and know which dates or issues the owner considers most relevant.

- Original prior records
- Vaccination history
- Laboratory and imaging reports
- Discharge or procedure notes
- Prior-clinic contact details

Sources: https://www.aaha.org/resources/2025-aaha-referral-guidelines/section-3-responsibilities-before-referral/

## Use a one-page handoff the team can scan

At the top, list the pet’s name, species, breed if known, age or birth date, sex, microchip number if relevant and owner contact details. Next, list the current reason for the visit in one sentence. Then add current medications, known reactions, the most recent relevant events and the owner’s three questions. Keep it to one page and attach—not replace—the official records.

This format respects the difference between owner observations and clinical findings. Write “limping after the evening walk on three dates” rather than “torn ligament.” Write “ate half the usual breakfast for two mornings” rather than assigning a cause. The page should help the veterinary team orient quickly while leaving diagnosis and treatment to the clinician.

- Pet identifiers
- One-sentence visit reason
- Current medication list
- Dated recent events
- Three owner priorities

Sources: https://www.aaha.org/resources/life-stage-canine-2019/tips-for-efficient-wellness-visits/

## Separate medications, supplements and prior reactions

List prescriptions, preventives, over-the-counter products and supplements separately. For each, capture the product name, strength, amount, frequency, last dose and label instructions. Include products that seem routine; a new hospital cannot safely assume what is used at home or what remains current in another system.

Give prior reactions their own section with the product or event, date, observed response and what the prior veterinarian advised. Do not label a reaction as an allergy unless that is how it appears in the medical record. AAHA advises bringing medications and supplements so dose and instructions can be verified from the prescription label.

- Prescription list
- Preventive products
- Supplements
- Label photographs
- Documented reaction history

Sources: https://www.aaha.org/resources/2025-aaha-referral-guidelines/section-3-responsibilities-before-referral/, https://vmc.vet.osu.edu/resources/assembling-emergency-kit-your-pets

## Build a timeline that preserves context

For a new concern, record when it began, how often it occurs, how long an episode lasts, what the pet was doing immediately before and whether food, water, bathroom habits, mobility, breathing, sleep or behavior changed. Preserve original photos or videos with timestamps. If the condition is intermittent, a short unedited clip may show context that is absent during the appointment.

Do not keep collecting content if the pet appears to need prompt care. Contact a veterinary professional and follow the routing instructions provided. The timeline is a communication aid, not a reason to delay evaluation or an online substitute for it.

- First date
- Frequency
- Duration
- Before-and-after context
- Original media

Sources: https://www.aaha.org/resources/life-stage-canine-2019/tips-for-efficient-wellness-visits/

## Ask for an arrival plan that fits the pet

Tell the hospital about fear, escape attempts, handling sensitivity, motion sickness or past aggression before arrival. Ask whether the pet should wait in the car, use a particular entrance or bring a familiar item. Use a secure carrier or leash and follow the hospital’s instructions about food and medication rather than relying on general internet advice.

AAHA recommends gradual, positive preparation and communication with the veterinary team when a dog or cat experiences intense anxiety. That can include carrier familiarity, short practice trips and familiar comfort items where appropriate. Medication decisions belong to the veterinarian and should never be improvised from a content preview.

- Fear and handling history
- Secure carrier or leash
- Entrance and waiting instructions
- Clinic-approved food and medication plan

Sources: https://www.aaha.org/resources/preparing-your-pet-for-a-successful-veterinary-visit/

## Leave with a follow-up map

Before the visit ends, ask who communicates results, how the hospital sends written instructions, what changes should prompt a call and when the next decision point occurs. Repeat the plan back in plain language. Keep the after-visit summary with the original one-page handoff so the record shows what changed after the new team evaluated the pet.

A useful hospital content system can reinforce that process through a clear follow-up page, but it must not turn generic education into individualized medical instruction. ## Frequently asked questions

### Should I summarize my pet’s old records for a new hospital?

Create a one-page owner handoff, but do not replace the source record. Ask the prior clinic to send relevant notes, tests, imaging and vaccination history. Keep your observations clearly labeled and let the receiving veterinary team interpret the clinical record.

### What belongs on a pet medication list?

Include prescriptions, preventives, nonprescription products and supplements with name, strength, amount, frequency, last dose and label instructions. Add clear label photos and separately note documented prior reactions. Follow the hospital’s directions before changing the schedule.

### What if my pet is very afraid of veterinary visits?

Tell the hospital before arrival and ask for handling, entrance and waiting instructions. Use a secure carrier or leash and practice positive, gradual familiarity where appropriate. Medication or sedation decisions must come from the veterinarian, not from a general article.

### Is an online new-patient request a confirmed appointment?

No. The hospital must accept the request and provide the date, time and preparation instructions. A routine form should not be used for emergencies, and a content preview should not imply availability that the hospital has not confirmed.
